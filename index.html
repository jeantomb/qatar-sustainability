The resource picture

Three resources.
One connected system.

Qatar's energy, water and food systems depend on one another. A solution that improves one resource can create new demands elsewhere.

The central question

Can technological innovation make Qatar's resource systems more secure — and more sustainable?

This investigation examines two real approaches: utility-scale solar electricity and hydroponic, controlled-environment agriculture.

Two responses

Changing supply.
Changing production.

Primary solution

Al Kharsaah Solar Power Plant

An 800 MWp solar photovoltaic plant that changes part of the electricity supply from gas to sunlight. In 2023 it generated 1,882 GWh, or 3.35% of Qatar's total generation.

Secondary solution

Hydroponics and high-tech agriculture

Soil-less growing systems and climate-managed greenhouses can use water more precisely and support domestic vegetable production, but cooling and electricity remain important constraints.

A geography investigation into resource sustainability in Qatar.
01 / Resource context

Understanding Qatar.

Qatar's location, arid climate and rapid population growth shape how the country produces energy, secures water and supplies food.

Geography

A peninsula on the Arabian Gulf.

Qatar projects north from the Arabian Peninsula into the Persian Gulf. Its only land border is with Saudi Arabia to the south. The country covers about 11,600 km² and has a low-lying, mostly flat desert landscape.

The Gulf is central to Qatar's resource system: seawater is desalinated to supply drinking water, while offshore gas reserves — including the North Field — underpin the country's energy economy. Qatar has no permanent rivers or lakes, so natural freshwater is limited to rainfall and groundwater.

Geography creates the challenge: little natural freshwater, extreme heat and limited land for conventional agriculture.
Sources: U.S. EIA (2025); Abu Hawash (2025); Worldometer (2025).

Climate

Heat, scarce rainfall and evaporation.

Extreme heat

Qatar recorded about 6,470 cooling degree days in 2020, compared with 128 heating degree days. Air conditioning is essential for much of the year.

Little natural water

Rainfall is low and irregular, usually concentrated in winter showers. There are no permanent rivers or lakes.

Limited recharge

Average natural groundwater recharge was about 54.2 million m³ per year over 1998–2021. Evaporation far exceeds rainfall.

Sources: CEIC / World Bank climate data; Abu Hawash (2025).

02 / The sustainability challenge

Pressure on essential resources.

Energy, water and food security are not separate issues. Qatar's climate and development patterns connect them in a single resource system.

01 / Energy

Electricity depends on natural gas.

Energy
What is the problem?

Fossil-fuel dependence

Qatar generated 56,250 GWh of electricity in 2023. Natural gas supplied about 96.65%, while solar supplied 3.35%. Power and water plants burned 464.7 million MMBtu of gas.

Why in Qatar?

Heat and demand

Gas is abundant and historically central to the economy. High temperatures make air conditioning essential. Estimates of air conditioning's electricity share vary from roughly one-third to two-thirds depending on the source and definition.

Consequences

Emissions and exposure

Burning gas releases greenhouse gases. Qatar's CO₂ emissions were reported at 41.27 tonnes per person in 2024 by Our World in Data. Subsidised energy can also weaken incentives to reduce consumption.

A seasonal pressure

Peak demand is almost three times the January minimum.

Peak electricity demand reached 9,805 MW in August 2023, compared with 3,270 MW in January. Cooling demand contributes to the sharp summer peak.

Source: Kahramaa (2024), monthly peak-demand data; ratio calculated by dividing August peak by January minimum.
02 / Water

Desalination meets demand, but the aquifer is under pressure.

Water
What is the problem?

Scarce natural freshwater

Qatar has little rainfall and no permanent rivers. Desalination produced 669.42 million m³ of water in 2023, while groundwater abstraction was estimated at about 200 million m³.

Why in Qatar?

Arid climate and farming

Natural groundwater recharge averages about 54.2 million m³ a year. Agriculture accounted for about 180 million m³ of the 200 million m³ abstracted in 2023.

Consequences

Energy, brine and depletion

Thermal desalination uses heat, often from gas. Brine is discharged to the Gulf and needs careful management. Groundwater abstraction exceeds average recharge, increasing pressure on quantity and quality.

03 / Food

Food security in a dry environment.

Food
What is the problem?

Import dependence

Around 2018, Qatar imported about 90% of its food requirements. Local production has expanded, but vegetables reached 39% self-sufficiency in 2024.

Why in Qatar?

Land and water limits

Arid conditions, high temperatures, limited fertile land and increasingly saline groundwater make conventional farming difficult. Vegetable production is concentrated in the cooler winter season.

Consequences

Supply-chain vulnerability

Imports expose Qatar to transport costs and disruptions. Before the 2017 blockade, more than 40% of food imports arrived overland through Saudi Arabia, revealing a vulnerability in supply routes.

The three challenges reinforce one another. Desalination and cooling require energy; farming requires water; and controlled-environment agriculture may require electricity for pumping and cooling.
The sustainability challenge is not simply to produce more. It is to secure resources while reducing the environmental cost of supplying them.

Primary solution

How the technology works

From sunlight to electricity.

Solar photovoltaic (PV) cells use semiconductor materials to convert sunlight directly into direct-current electricity. Inverters convert this into alternating current, which can be supplied to the electricity grid.

Al Kharsaah uses bifacial panels that can collect light from both sides, single-axis trackers that follow the sun, and automated robotic cleaning systems designed to limit dust accumulation.

Sources: TotalEnergies (n.d.); Power Technology (n.d.).

The Al Kharsaah project

A major first step.

The plant was Qatar's first large-scale solar project and demonstrated that utility-scale solar can operate in the country's desert environment.

Location and timeline

Al Kharsaah, west of Doha

The 10 km² site is about 80 km west of Doha. Project agreements were signed in January 2020. The first phase began supplying the grid in June 2022, and the plant was inaugurated on 18 October 2022.

Ownership: QatarEnergy Renewable Solutions (60%), Marubeni (20.4%) and TotalEnergies (19.6%). Kahramaa purchases the electricity under a 25-year agreement.

Sources: Kahramaa (2020); The Peninsula (2022); TotalEnergies (n.d.).

The wider transition

More solar capacity is planned.

Two further QatarEnergy solar plants at Ras Laffan (458 MW) and Mesaieed (417 MW) began operating in April 2025. A 2,000 MW Dukhan project is planned for 2029.

Qatar's stated ambition is 4 GW of utility-scale renewable capacity by 2030, with renewables supplying about 18% of electricity production.

Environmental benefits

Lower operating emissions

Solar panels generate electricity without burning fuel during operation. Each unit of solar electricity can displace some gas-fired generation and its associated emissions.

Economic benefits

Diversification

Solar broadens the electricity mix and may free some natural gas for export or other uses. A long-term power purchase agreement provides a route to market.

Limitations

Scale and timing

Solar generated only 3.35% of electricity in 2023. Its share was lowest in August and September, when electricity demand was highest. Storage and grid integration matter.

The developer-side estimate of about 26 million tonnes of CO₂ avoided over the plant's lifetime is a projection, not a measured result. Installed capacity (800 MWp) is not the same as annual electricity generation (1,882 GWh).
Primary solution

Solar changes the source of the problem.

Electricity generation depends heavily on gas. Solar power tackles this at the supply stage by replacing a portion of gas-fired generation with renewable electricity. It is therefore a primary solution by design — although its contribution was still relatively small in 2023.

What is hydroponics?

Growing without conventional soil.

Hydroponics is a method of growing plants without natural soil. Roots receive a carefully balanced nutrient solution, either directly in water or through an inert growing medium such as coco coir, perlite or rock wool.

Water and nutrients can be delivered precisely to the roots, and surplus solution can be collected and recirculated. This can reduce water losses compared with some open-field irrigation methods.

Controlled environments

Managing heat and humidity.

Greenhouses can protect crops from harsh outdoor conditions and allow growers to control temperature, humidity and irrigation.

In the Al Khor trials, multi-span plastic greenhouses used evaporative cooling: incoming air passes over wet pads, lowering its temperature. The trial team monitored climate conditions, nutrients and crop growth, then shared the results with Qatari farmers (QAFCO, 2024).

Water efficiency

Targeted delivery

Recirculation can reduce water lost through drainage and evaporation, depending on crop and system design.

Food resilience

Local production

Producing more vegetables locally can diversify supply and reduce exposure to international transport disruption.

Trade-off

Electricity demand

Pumps and cooling require energy. In Qatar, much of that electricity is still generated from natural gas.

Benefits

Less water, more local supply

Hydroponics can improve water productivity and raise yields per square metre. Local vegetable production can reduce reliance on imported produce and shorten supply chains.

Limitations

Cost, energy and reach

Greenhouses need investment, technical skills and electricity for cooling and pumps. Groundwater remains widely used by farms, and an earlier 70% greenhouse vegetable target for 2023 was not achieved.

Secondary solution

Hydroponics manages the effects of scarcity.

Hydroponics can reduce pressure on imported food and improve water-use efficiency, but it does not remove Qatar's underlying climate constraints: extreme heat, low rainfall and limited natural freshwater. Its benefits also depend on the energy used to cool and operate greenhouses.

05 / Evaluation

How far can technology take Qatar?

Solar energy and hydroponics address different links in the resource chain. Their benefits are real, but neither can solve every sustainability challenge alone.

An important connection

The solutions can strengthen one another.

Greenhouses need electricity for pumping and cooling. If more of that electricity came from solar rather than gas, the energy-related impact of hydroponics could fall. This is a logical connection between the two technologies, not a measured result from a Qatari project.

The big question

To what extent can Qatar achieve greater resource sustainability through technological innovation?

Progress has been real. Desalination has provided a reliable water supply to a country with almost no natural freshwater: 669 million m³ was produced in 2023. District cooling plants increased from 39 to 70 between 2019 and 2023, and Kahramaa reports that district cooling can reduce electricity use by 30–40% compared with conventional cooling. Al Kharsaah demonstrated that utility-scale solar can operate in Qatar, while further solar plants added 875 MW in 2025. Greenhouse vegetable self-sufficiency rose from about 32% in 2018 to 42% in 2023.

However, technology alone has limits. Solar supplied only 3.35% of electricity in 2023 while peak demand rose 15.7% in four years. Hydroponics' water savings can come with an energy cost, and the earlier greenhouse target was missed. Desalination depends on energy and creates brine; groundwater is still abstracted at roughly 146 million m³ a year more than average natural recharge. High consumption also means that better supply does not automatically create sustainability.

Qatar therefore needs to reduce demand as well as improve supply. Better building design, efficient cooling, water conservation, lower network losses, treated wastewater for agriculture, solar paired with storage, diversified food import routes and strategic reserves can all contribute. Protecting groundwater as an emergency reserve is also important.

Overall judgement

To a considerable but limited extent.

Technology can transform how Qatar supplies resources, but lasting sustainability depends on how much Qatar needs to supply in the first place.

“Qatar has shown that technology can bend the curve. It has not yet shown that the curve has bent far enough.”
